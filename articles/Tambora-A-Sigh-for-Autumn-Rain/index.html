Three Poets, One Title

“A Sigh for Autumn Rain”( 秋雨叹) : The version most educated Chinese readers know is from Du Fu. He actually wrote three poems under this title. Here's the translation of the third.

In Chang’an where ordinary folk hardly matter 长安布衣谁比数
Locked in by wooden doors 反锁衡门守环堵
An old man can’t grow basil 老夫不出长蓬蒿
A child who walks in the wind and rain worries not 稚子无忧走风雨
The morning rain brings a rush of cold air 雨声飕飕催早寒
Northern geese with wet wings finds it hard to fly 胡雁翅湿高飞难
Autumn comes and I haven’t seen the light of day 秋来未曾见白日
Mud and mire, when will it dry 泥污后土何时干

Du Fu, Chang’an, 754

Lu You (Southern Song, 1125–1210)'s version "点点滴滴雨到明，凄凄恻恻梦不成" (The raindrops fall steadily until dawn, a sorrowful dream remains unfulfilled.) is less widely known but still familiar to many.

When I read Tambora: The Eruption That Changed the World by Gillen D'Arcy Wood I was surprised to find another “A Sigh for Autumn Rain" by an unknown poet Li Yuyang 李于阳(1784–1826):

The clouds like a dragon’s breath on the mountains,
Winds howl, circling and swirling,
The Rain God shakes the stars, and the rain
Beats down on the world. An earthquake of rain.
Water spilling from the eaves deafens me.
People rush from falling houses in their thousands
And tens of thousands, for the work of the rain
Is worse than the work of thieves. Bricks crack. Walls fall.
In an instant, the house is gone. My child catches my coat
And cries out. I am running in the muddy road, then
Back to rescue my money and grains from the ruins.
What else to do? My loved ones must eat.
There are no words for the bitterness of
An empty September. The flood-drowned fields
harvest three grains for every ten of a good year.
And from these three grains? Meals and clothes till next September.”

Du Fu's “A Sigh for Autumn Rain” (754) and Li's version (c. 1816–1817) share a similar melancholic vibe—the despair and endless rain—despite spanning more than a thousand years.

But no matter how hard I tried I couldn't find the original Chinese version of Li Yuyang's poem. I can only see that Wood thanks the librarians at Yunnan University for their help during the 2011 research trip, as well as Professor Rong Guangqi of Wuhan University for his first-draft translations of Li Yuyang’s poetry in his acknowledgements.

Tambora-Yunnan famine connection

The year 1816 after Tambora's eruption was called a year without summer.

One of the earliest Chinese scholarly works to explicitly link the 1815 Tambora eruption to the severe 1815–1817 famine in Yunnan (the Jiaqing Great Famine) came from Fudan University researchers. It was published (in Chinese) in 2005 in the Fudan Journal (Social Sciences):

Abstract : The three years' famine (1815 —1817) in Jiaqing Reign Period of Qing Dynasty is a most serious disaster in the recorded Yunnan pre-modern history. The typical microthermal climate in summer and autumn resulted in a serious reduction of the production of paddy , buckwheat and other crops. In 1816 , the summit ofthe typical microthermal climate , the average temperature in August was 2. 5—3 dgree lower than in other years in Kunming. This thesis has illustrated that this microthermal climate disaster was mainly due to the cloud from the eruption of Tambora Volcano , which weakened the solar radiation energy that reached the earth. Most areas in China were involved in this microthermal climate , but disaster was not widespread in eastern part.

In the notes to Chapter Five, “The Seven Sorrows of Yunnan,” Wood wrote,

The inspiration for this chapter lies with the original research of Yang Yuda of the University of Fudan in Shanghai, in particular his lengthy article coauthored with Man Zhmin and Zheng Jinyun, “The Great Yunnan Famine of the Jiaqing Period (1815–17) and the Eruption of the Volcano Mount Tambora,” Fudan Journal: Social Sciences (2005): 79–85 (unpublished translation by Nicholas Brown). Two other recent Chinese sources provided supplementary information: Shuji Cao, “The Tambora Eruption and Chinese Social History,” Academics in China 5 (September 2009): 37–41 (unpublished translation by Fang Wan); and Bantian Chen, “The Serious Famine in Yunnan during the Jiaqing Period (1796–1820),” Shi Jie Bo Lan [Global Vision] 8 (2010): 76–79 (unpublished translation by Fang Wan).

Wood’s Chapter Five is also the main English-language account that brings Li Yuyang’s poetry into this climatic history. The literary and historical connections he draws there—especially the revival of the “Seven Sorrows” poetic tradition—deeply resonated with me.

Yunnan Opium Cultivation: A Timeline Problem

That said, I believe Wood made a mistake regarding the timeline and scale of opium cultivation in Yunnan and the opium connection with the Tambora eruption. In my email exchange with him I tried to be polite by saying "I feel compelled to share one point of concern regarding the timeline and scale of opium cultivation in Yunnan and the opium connection with the Tambora eruption". But the truth is he was wrong about it.

In the text, he describes (paraphrased):

“Beginning in 1820, however, only two years after the end of the Tambora-driven famine, Chinese rulers in Peking were startled to receive reports from faraway Yunnan of a sudden explosion in opium production there… by 1840—during the first Opium War with Britain—Yunnan was the acknowledged heart of domestic opium production in China.”

This timeline and the description of a “sudden explosion” in 1820, with Yunnan already the “acknowledged heart” by 1840, do not align with the mainstream historical scholarship on opium cultivation in Yunnan—particularly in light of the book's opening question in Chapter Five: “What caused Yunnan’s sudden transformation, in less than two decades, from a grain-producing province well integrated with the empire’s agricultural system to a rogue narco-state in thrall to the international drug trade?” and the subsequent claim that what follows is a “probable scenario” driven by the Tambora famine. While the famine of 1816–1818 likely served as an important accelerator for small farmers' shift toward high-value cash crops like opium, the major commercial takeoff and Yunnan's emergence as the dominant domestic producer occurred much later, primarily in the post-Opium Wars era (especially the 1870s–1890s).

A More Accurate Chronology

Here is a more accurate timeline based on established Chinese and international research:

  1. Pre-1840 (including the Tambora period 1816–1818)
    Opium poppy cultivation in Yunnan was limited to scattered, small-scale planting, mainly in remote border areas among ethnic minority communities (e.g., near Burma and Vietnam). It was mostly for local use or small trade. Official reports surfaced in 1823, prompting the first formal imperial anticultivation orders for the Yun-Gui region, but case records remained sparse through the 1820s. By 1830–1832, Shao Zhenghu’s memorial and reports from Yun-Gui Governor-General Ruan Yuan noted that “70 to 80 percent” of Yunnan’s product had been eradicated while acknowledging persistence in remote mountains; both border ethnic groups and inland Han were planting for profit. In 1838–1840 intensified prohibition produced substantial seizures: Bello’s data record roughly 337,074 taels (~12,573 kilos) confiscated in Yunnan between January 1839 and April 1840, with native chieftainships accounting for about 8 percent. In 1839 Censor Lu Yinggu observed that opium reaching the Sichuan–Guizhou border “comes mainly from Yunnan, where the cultivation of poppy for the decoction of opium has been going on for many years.” Contemporary reports described southwestern production as more extensive than in coastal provinces by the early 1830s, with cheaper local afurong beginning to flow north and east, and profits sometimes said to reach “ten times” that of rice. These developments show a commercially relevant regional industry by the late 1830s, but there was no “sudden explosion” in 1820, nor was Yunnan yet the “acknowledged heart” of Chinese domestic production or a major concern on the scale later claimed.

  2. 1840s–1860s (post-Opium Wars)
    After the First Opium War (1840–1842) and Second Opium War (1856–1860), imported foreign opium flooded China, causing severe silver outflow. This led to the policy shift toward “yitu diyang” 以土抵洋 (using domestic opium to offset foreign imports). Yunnan planting began to grow noticeably during this period, but it was still secondary to provinces such as Sichuan, Guizhou, Shaanxi, and Gansu.

  3. 1870s–1890s (Guangxu (光绪) reign peak)
    Under the “yujin yuzheng” 寓禁于征 policy (nominal prohibition combined with taxation), Yunnan’s opium industry entered its golden age. Yunnan “Yuntu” (云土) became the most famous and highest-quality domestic opium. Qin Heping estimates planting area at roughly 300,000 mu in 1893–1898 and 700,000 mu in 1899–1908 (with some peak figures reaching toward 1,000,000 mu). By 1906 Yunnan produced about 78,000 piculs, second to Sichuan’s approximately 238,000 piculs. Zhong Weimin notes that by the late nineteenth century roughly one-third of Yunnan’s cultivated land was under poppy in peak estimates, and domestic output largely met internal demand. By the late 19th century Yunnan was widely recognized as one of the largest and most important domestic opium-producing provinces in China.

Conclusion

While the Tambora-induced famine of 1816–1818 likely acted as an important accelerator for the shift to high-value cash crops (including opium) among Yunnan’s small farmers, the “sudden explosion” and Yunnan’s status as the “acknowledged heart” of Chinese domestic opium production did not occur in the 1820s, nor was it fully established by 1840. These developments belong mainly to the post-Opium Wars period, especially the 1870s–1890s. The 1815–1817 Jiaqing famine was severe and is linked by Chinese scholarship (notably Yang Yuda et al., Fudan Journal, 2005) to the Tambora eruption, but specialist opium historians emphasize price differentials with Indian opium, frontier administrative weaknesses, and later policy shifts as the primary drivers of commercial expansion.
